cell cycle

Prophase (stage 1)

Prophase is the first and longest stage of mitosis, the process where a single cell divides into two identical daughter cells. During this critical phase, the loosely packed chromatin inside the nucleus condenses into highly visible, tightly coiled chromosomes, each consisting of two identical sister chromatids joined at a centromere. As this occurs, the nucleolus disappears, and the nuclear envelope begins to break down, allowing the cellular machinery access to the genetic material. Outside the nucleus, structures called centrosomes move toward opposite poles of the cell and begin radiating protein fibers to form the mitotic spindle. This spindle apparatus will ultimately attach to the chromosomes and orchestrate their movement, setting the stage for accurate genetic separation in the subsequent phases of cell division.

metaphase

Metaphase is the second and most highly ordered stage of cell division, occurring in both mitosis and meiosis, where the cell ensures its genetic material is perfectly aligned before separation. During this critical phase, the nuclear membrane has already completely broken down, allowing the cell's duplicated chromosomes to migrate to the center of the cell. Spindle fibers, which radiate from structures called centrosomes at opposite poles of the cell, attach securely to the protein complexes known as kinetochores on each chromosome. Under tension from these fibers, the chromosomes line up single-file along an imaginary equator called the metaphase plate. This precise alignment is strictly monitored by the cell's spindle assembly checkpoint, a quality control mechanism that halts division if even a single chromosome is misaligned, preventing genetic errors before the cell transitions into anaphase.

anaphase

Anaphase is the third and fastest stage of cell division (mitosis and meiosis), during which replicated chromosomes are separated and moved to opposite poles of the cell. Triggered by the degradation of the cellular "glue" known as cohesin, the sister chromatids are pulled apart by the shortening of spindle fibers attached to their kinetochores. Concurrently, non-kinetochore spindle fibers elongate, pushing the cell poles further apart and stretching the entire cell into an oval shape. This precise segregation ensures that each resulting daughter cell receives an identical and complete set of genetic material, setting the stage for the final restructuring of the cell during telophase.
